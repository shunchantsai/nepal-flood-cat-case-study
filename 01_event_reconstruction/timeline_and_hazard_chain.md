# Event Reconstruction & Hazard Chain

## What is confirmed vs. still under investigation

**Confirmed:**
- A large, rapid slope failure occurred at Lhende Khola, near the Nepal-China (Tibet) border, on 26 August 2026 at approximately 08:37 local time.
- USGS determined the resulting seismic signal (equivalent to ~M5.2) was generated by the slope failure itself, not a tectonic earthquake. A second, smaller signal (~M4.2) followed roughly 3 hours later, also attributed to slope instability.
- Flood waters travelled the ~36 km from the border area to Betrawati in approximately 40 minutes.
- At the Trishuli River's Galchhi gauge, water level rose by roughly 9 metres in about 30 minutes; at Malekhu, roughly 7 metres in a similar window.
- The same general corridor experienced a comparable-scale event 14 months earlier (the July 2025 Purepu glacial lake outburst flood, sourced from the Tibet side); this event's failure point is assessed as being on the Nepal side.

**Still under investigation (treat as working hypothesis, not fact):**
- Whether the failed material formed a temporary landslide/ice dam on Lhende Khola that subsequently breached, amplifying the downstream flood wave.
- The precise composition of the initial mass (ice, rock, or a mix) and whether a supraglacial or moraine-dammed lake was involved.

## Working hazard chain

```text
High-altitude rock/ice slope failure (Lhende Khola, ~5,000+ m elevation)
        ↓
Debris (ice, rock, sediment) enters the drainage
        ↓
Possible temporary landslide dam forms and breaches (unconfirmed)
        ↓
Debris flood travels down Bhote Koshi into the Trishuli River system
        ↓
Corridor infrastructure impact within ~40 minutes: border crossing,
roads, bridges, settlements, hydropower facilities
```

## Why this corridor is structurally high-risk

- **Narrow, steep terrain**: the valley leaves little room for energy to dissipate.
- **Cross-border basin**: the failure point and the exposed downstream assets sit in different jurisdictions, complicating monitoring and early warning.
- **Infrastructure concentration**: the border crossing, ~19+ bridges, ~40+ km of road, and a string of hydropower facilities are all sited along the same narrow corridor — a single event can sever transport, power, and border trade simultaneously.
- **Monitoring vulnerability**: upstream gauges were themselves damaged or swept away during the event, degrading real-time visibility exactly when it was most needed.
- **Repeat-event signal**: two major events in the same corridor within 14 months raises the question of whether historical return-period assumptions (e.g., "50-year flood") for this specific corridor are still appropriate — see `04_scenarios/`.

## Useful analog (not a claim about this event's mechanism)

The 2021 Chamoli, India disaster (Shugar et al., 2021, *Science*) involved a large rock/ice avalanche that produced a seismic signal initially attributed to an earthquake, before rapidly transitioning into a highly mobile debris flow that destroyed two hydropower plants. It is cited here as a mechanism precedent — large slope failures can generate misleading seismic signatures — not as evidence about how this specific event unfolded.

## Casualty and rescue status (illustrative snapshot — update with latest official figures)

As of the most recent figures in the source material (~5 September 2026):
- 1,344 bodies recovered; ~5,000 people still reported missing; 13,098 people rescued (Nepal side)
- ~900 hydropower workers reported missing, of whom ~500 were believed possibly trapped in tunnels
- Tibet side: 31 dead, 531 missing (figures from separate national authorities, not yet reconciled)
- Rescues continued more than a week after the event, including a worker extracted after 10 days trapped roughly 225 m into the Upper Trishuli-1 tunnel

**Note:** all casualty figures are dynamic and were still being reconciled across agencies at time of writing. Cite the date of any figure used and note it as provisional.
